# Opinion

**Opinion**

The claimant has been symptomatic for approximately three years without
substantive improvement in the recent past. She has received structured
and multidisciplinary interventions, but her current plateau at 5 to 10
hours of work per week reflects a stable and low functional tolerance.
Recovery beyond this level is unlikely. In similar chronic post-viral
and long-COVID syndromes, clinically meaningful gains generally occur
within the first 12 to 18 months. After that period, most individuals
establish a fixed baseline that remains stable rather than showing
progressive improvement. The available evidence indicates that the
probability of a full-time functional recovery after three years of
persistent fatigue and cognitive and physical limitations is very low to
extremely low.

Further formal rehabilitation is unlikely to provide additional benefit.
The claimant has already completed extensive therapies, and the
principles of pacing, energy conservation, and gradual re-engagement
have been established. In the absence of reversible pathology, the
expected improvement from additional structured therapy would be
marginal and unlikely to result in a substantial functional change. It
therefore likely does not justify the associated cost.

At this stage, a self-directed maintenance plan is more appropriate. The
claimant can continue her current pacing and conditioning routines
independently, with occasional monitoring by her primary care provider
or physiotherapist. Reassessment should focus on stability rather than
pursuing an active rehabilitation plan with an expectation of
substantial recovery or full-time return to work. Transitioning away
from formal therapies toward self-management is consistent with current
best practice for chronic post-viral fatigue syndromes.

The claimant herself has expressed satisfaction with part-time activity
and has not indicated any interest in returning to full-time work.
Sustained motivation and goal orientation are strong predictors of
functional recovery, whereas self-limiting expectations tend to
reinforce existing activity restrictions. Given her stated preference
for a reduced schedule and the absence of progressive gains in the past
year, the likelihood of regaining full-time capacity is very low.
Continued formal therapy would not be expected to overcome motivational
or tolerance-based limits that have become fixed over time, as appears
to be the case here.

The recent diagnostic findings of dysautonomia consistent with POTS and
mild visual disturbance on VNG are not expected to materially alter her
prognosis or functional capacity. These results are consistent with the
established post-viral fatigue process and do not represent new or
highly treatable conditions. While such abnormalities may contribute to
intermittent dizziness or visual strain, they do not explain the
persistent fatigue, cognitive slowing, or post-exertional intolerance
that limits her work capacity. Management of POTS through hydration,
compression, and conditioning has already been discussed. Further
targeted interventions would not be expected to restore sustained
cognitive or sedentary work tolerance.

In summary, further insurance-funded rehabilitation or multidisciplinary
programming aimed at restoring her prior full functional level or
full-time employment is not warranted. Her condition has stabilized into
a chronic pattern consistent with partial total disability, with
plateaued physical and cognitive tolerance. Additional structured
interventions are unlikely to alter her overall prognosis or work
capacity.

While an independent medical examination or functional capacity
evaluation could be considered for documentation or administrative
purposes, such assessments would likely yield limited new information
given the chronicity and consistency of findings. Objective testing may
confirm reduced endurance and pacing limitations but is not expected to
identify new therapeutic options or materially change management.
Ongoing maintenance through self-directed strategies therefore remains
the most appropriate approach.

The claimant is currently engaged in self-employment within her own
vocational rehabilitation business, working flexible hours between five
and ten per week. This confirms a measurable, though reduced, work
capacity and supports the conclusion that her impairment represents a
partial disability rather than a total loss of function. Given this
demonstrated residual capacity, the applicable policy definition of
disability may need to be reviewed to determine whether her current
self-directed part-time activity aligns with the criteria for continued
benefit entitlement.
